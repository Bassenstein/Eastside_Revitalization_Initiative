What is CDBG?

The Community Development Block Grant is one of the federal government's oldest and most flexible tools for helping cities improve their own neighborhoods.

Created

1974

Established by the Housing & Community Development Act of 1974 (Public Law 93-383), run by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD).

Who gets it

Entitlement Cities

Larger cities and counties — like Cleveland — automatically qualify for a grant every year based on a formula. It isn't a competitive application.

How it's sized

A Formula

HUD calculates each city's grant using measures like poverty rate, population, overcrowded housing, age of housing, and growth lag.

In plain terms: CDBG money flows to Cleveland City Hall every year, not to individual residents or nonprofits directly. The City then decides — with public input — which local projects it pays for.

Every dollar has to clear a bar

CDBG isn't a blank check. HUD requires every activity to meet one of three "National Objectives" — and at least 70% of all funds must fall into the first one.

Objective 1 · The 70% Rule

Benefit Low/Moderate-Income People

Either a whole area is majority lower-income ("area benefit"), or the activity serves a specific low-income group, low-income housing, or creates jobs for low-income workers.

Objective 2

Eliminate Slum or Blight

Removing conditions that are unsafe, deteriorated, or drag down a block or a single "spot" property — demolition, cleanup, and stabilization all qualify.

Objective 3 · Rare

Meet an Urgent Need

Used only when an existing condition poses a serious, immediate threat to health or safety and no other money is available — think disaster response.

What CDBG can actually pay for

Once an activity clears a national objective, HUD allows a wide range of uses. Here's the plain-English version.

Eligible

Public facilities & improvements

Streets, sidewalks, water/sewer lines, parks, community centers, lighting.

Eligible

Clearance & demolition

Tearing down or cleaning up unsafe, blighted structures and lots.

Eligible

Housing rehab

Repairing owner-occupied and rental homes for lower-income residents.

Eligible

Economic development

Loans or assistance to businesses that create or retain jobs for local residents.

Eligible, capped

Public services

Job training, senior & youth programs, health services — generally capped near 15% of the grant.

Eligible, capped

Planning & administration

Staff time, compliance, monitoring — capped at 20% of the grant.

The rules that trip good projects up

Being "eligible" isn't the finish line. These are the compliance tests that decide whether a well-meant project actually survives a HUD review — and where community proposals most often fall down.

LMI benefit floor70%
Public services cap15%
Admin & planning cap20%
Area-benefit threshold51%+
Document it

Slum/blight must be designated, not asserted

For an area claim, the City must draw the boundaries, cite a State or local blight definition, and show a substantial share of properties (HUD applies a 25% test) meet defined conditions — re-checked every 10 years. Saying lots "are blight" isn't enough.

Maintenance ≠ eligible

One-time fix, yes; mowing forever, no

CDBG pays for the initial improvement — clearance, grading, turf establishment. Ongoing maintenance and routine mowing are the City's cost, not the grant's.

Helping a business

Public-benefit & "but-for" tests

Assistance to a for-profit must be the minimum needed to make a deal work, and clear public-benefit limits — no more than $50,000 in CDBG per job created, or $1,000 per person served.

Before the first dollar

Environmental review & prevailing wage

An environmental review (24 CFR Part 58) must clear before funds are committed, and Davis-Bacon prevailing wages apply to most construction work.

FOS216 Ask #2

Create a real, coordinated Blight Task Force — Cleveland currently doesn't have one.

Nobody's actually in charge of blight

Blight response today is split across six City departments plus the county Environmental Crimes Task Force, with no single entity coordinating any of it.

Building & Housing Public Works Urban Forestry City Planning Cleveland Land Bank Public Safety / Police County Environmental Crimes Task Force
  1. No centralized authoritySlow response times and duplicated effort because no one department owns blight elimination end-to-end.
  2. No parcel-level trackingThe City has no shared GIS system scoring lots for debris, sidewalk failure, tree hazards, and dumping.
  3. Dumping enforcement sits aloneThe Environmental Crimes Task Force investigates illegal dumping, but cleanup is handled by a completely separate team.

The ask: tie CDBG-funded lot stabilization work directly to code enforcement, forestry, and the Environmental Crimes Task Force through shared GIS maps, joint inspections, and monthly coordination calls — effectively standing up the task force Cleveland doesn't currently have.

How CDBG can legally pay for a grocery

CDBG can't hand cash to a grocery chain. But it can assemble the site, build the public improvements, and route gap financing through the right structure. Three eligible pathways, two guardrails.

Pathway · 570.203

Special Economic Development

Supports commercial projects that create jobs and expand access to essential goods — structured as a loan or forgivable loan tied to LMI hiring.

Pathway · 570.204

CBDO-carried project

A Community-Based Development Organization (a local CDC such as Famicos, Burten Bell Carr, or Fairfax Renaissance) acquires land, builds, and leases to the operator. A CBDO is not a "subrecipient" — it's its own category.

Pathway · 570.201(c)

Public Facilities & Improvements

Sidewalks, curbs, parking, utilities, lighting, and demolition of blighted storefronts around a future grocery site are directly eligible.

Guardrail

No direct cash grants

Money flows as a loan, forgivable loan, or CBDO-run project — never a straight grant to a private chain.

Guardrail · 570.209

Public-benefit & underwriting

Direct 570.203 assistance must pass the public-benefit test — no more than $50,000 per job, or $1,000 per person served — plus a "but-for" underwriting review.

The Program

Neighborhood Grocery Attraction Program (FOS216-NGAP-001) — an incentive package plus a replicable store prototype.

Make the deal pencil, then repeat it

Chains locate on the East Side when the numbers work. The program's job is to combine City, Land Bank, and financing tools into one package that closes the gap between what a store costs to open here and what it can earn — then standardize it so the next store is faster than the last.

  1. Assemble the siteThe City and Cuyahoga Land Bank deliver a corridor parcel assembly, cleared and ready — removing the single biggest barrier to urban grocery development.
  2. Fund the gap, don't gift itCDBG public improvements plus a gap loan or forgivable loan tied to LMI hiring — layered with HOME, ARPA, and Land Bank capital where eligible.
  3. Protect the marketCleveland already restricts new dollar / small-box stores within two miles of an existing one, protecting share for a real grocery entrant instead of another dollar store.

Precedent — it already works: Meijer built on East 105th Street, and Simon's Supermarket took over a former Giant Eagle in Buckeye Plaza. Neither happened by accident — both followed a City recruiting effort with a real incentive package behind it.

The Bridge Format store

A full-service grocery banner averages 38,000–43,000 SF — a suburban pad-site format that doesn't fit most East Side blocks. The Bridge Format sits between a corner bodega and a Walmart Neighborhood Market: a true full-service box sized to a 3–5 parcel corridor assembly.

Where it lands first

The prototype is site-agnostic, but two East Side opportunities are live right now — one a City-controlled building already built for the use, the other a six-acre assembly already underway.

Glenville · Active

New Eastside Market, St. Clair Ave

Closed March 31, 2026; the City took control of the building over unpaid taxes and utilities and is preparing an RFQ for a new grocery operator. A building already zoned and built for the use, sitting empty — the single most immediate opportunity.

Collinwood · Assembling

Former Dave's Supermarket site

Vacant since 2022. The City and Cuyahoga Land Bank are assembling roughly six acres around it with a private developer — officials have called it the kind of property that can "swing a neighborhood." A live target for a Bridge Format anchor or a full-format partner.

The through-line: stabilized lots (Ask #1), a coordinated blight authority (Ask #2), and grocery access (Ask #3) are one strategy for the same blocks — clear the ground, keep it clear, then put something on it people can walk to.
