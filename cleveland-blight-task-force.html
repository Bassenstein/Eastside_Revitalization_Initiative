Executive Summary

Cleveland does not have a blight authority. It has six City departments and a county task force that each own a piece of blight, no shared parcel record, no common severity standard, and no one accountable for the outcome. This document proposes the Cleveland Blight Task Force (CBTF) as the missing coordinating layer — and uses the Ward 8 CDBG program as its operational pilot.

The Problem
Fragmentation, not neglect

Building & Housing, Public Works, Urban Forestry, City Planning, the Land Bank, Public Safety, and the county Environmental Crimes Task Force all touch blight. None of them can direct another. A single blighted parcel can require four work orders from four systems, none of which know about each other.

The Instrument
A coordinating authority, not a new department

The CBTF is proposed as a standing task force under the Mayor's Office with a permanent operations cell — not a new department requiring charter amendment. It directs, sequences, and tracks work that existing departments perform, and owns the one thing no department currently owns: the parcel record.

The Precedent
Nine years of DeKalb data

DeKalb County, Georgia stood up an equivalent body in January 2017. It is the closest available benchmark for structure, staffing, and sequencing — including the parts Cleveland should not copy. Section 03 separates what transfers from what does not.

The Ask
  • Establish the Cleveland Blight Task Force by Mayoral Executive Order, ratified by Council ordinance within 120 days.
  • Fund a Year 1 operating budget of $2,602,800 (Section 11), blended from CDBG administration, general fund, and county solid-waste sources.
  • Adopt the Blight Severity Index (Section 07) as the city's standard parcel condition instrument, closing Gap 2.
  • Formalize the coordination protocol with the Cuyahoga County Environmental Crimes Task Force (Section 08), closing Gap 5.
  • Designate Ward 8 as the 18-month proving ground, using the CDBG program already scoped and budgeted (Section 09).

Finding — No Unified Blight Authority Exists

Blight responsibility in Cleveland is distributed across seven bodies, each with statutory authority over one slice of the problem and none with authority over the whole. The table below maps what each entity owns, and — critically — what falls between them.

What falls between the seams

No entity currently coordinates vacant-lot stabilization, sidewalk and curb remediation, forestry hazard mitigation, debris removal, illegal dumping prevention, parcel-level safety improvements, infrastructure restoration, or community-use preparation as a single sequenced operation. The result is measurable: slow response, duplicated mobilizations, and parcels that cycle back to blighted condition within a season of being cleared — a pattern concentrated in East Side neighborhoods including Hough.

The structural test

A blight authority exists when one named officer can answer three questions about any parcel in the city: what condition is it in, who is working on it, and when will it be resolved. No Cleveland officer can currently answer any of the three from a single system of record.

The Six Systemic Gaps

Carried forward from the Cleveland Blight Systems Gap Analysis. Each gap is paired below with the CBTF instrument that closes it.

01
No centralized blight authority

No single entity coordinates blight elimination across departments, producing slow response times, duplicated effort, and unresolved conditions.

Closed by → CBTF charter and standing operations cell (Sections 04–05).

02
No parcel-level blight tracking system

The city maintains no comprehensive GIS-based scoring matrix covering debris fields, sidewalk and curb failures, tree hazards, drainage issues, demolition scars, or dumping hotspots.

Closed by → Blight Severity Index + citywide parcel condition survey (Sections 07, 10).

03
Infrastructure blight is unaddressed

Sidewalks, curbs, driveway aprons, and demolition-damaged infrastructure are not systematically repaired, producing unsafe pedestrian conditions and inviting recurring dumping.

Closed by → Infrastructure condition weighted into the Index and bundled into single-mobilization work packages.

04
Forestry hazards are not integrated into blight response

Tree hazards are handled as isolated service requests rather than structural blight conditions. Overgrown tree lines conceal dumping and reduce sightlines.

Closed by → Forestry seat on the operations cell; canopy concealment scored as a Index criterion.

05
Illegal dumping enforcement is siloed

The Environmental Crimes Task Force investigates dumping while remediation proceeds separately through Public Works or contractors, with no shared case record.

Closed by → Formal coordination protocol and shared hotspot layer (Section 08).

06
No coordinated vacant-lot stabilization program

Cleveland lacks a standardized approach to grading, soil remediation, turf establishment, erosion control, drainage correction, and community-use preparation.

Closed by → Ward 8 stabilization standards adopted as the citywide specification (Section 09).

03

Precedent — DeKalb County, Georgia

DeKalb County established what is commonly referred to as its blight task force — formally the Demolition and Abatement Task Force — in January 2017 under then-CEO Michael Thurmond. It is the most useful available benchmark for Cleveland because it began from the same condition: blight authority scattered across departments, no parcel inventory, and enforcement disconnected from remediation.

Structure
Multi-departmental, standing, executive-sponsored

Created by executive action to share information, coordinate enforcement, and collaborate on blight-reduction initiatives. Representatives from Law, Planning & Sustainability, Community Development, Keep DeKalb Beautiful, Code Compliance, Sanitation, and GIS meet on a regular cycle — the same functional mix Cleveland would need.

Funding
Dedicated appropriation at launch

The county allocated $3.8 million in board-approved funds at the start of 2017 specifically to enhance blight-fighting initiatives. The task force did not run on borrowed departmental capacity — a distinction that matters for Cleveland's Year 1 budget request.

The Inventory Step
Comprehensive property condition survey

A countywide condition survey of unincorporated DeKalb was completed in September 2020 — the first time the county could identify every property subject to abatement or demolition. This is the direct analogue of Cleveland's Gap 2, and DeKalb's sequencing suggests the survey should come earlier, not later.

Judicial Pathway
Court order precedes private-property action

To remediate blight on private property, the county must first obtain a court order. DeKalb's Magistrate Court and Solicitor-General's office built dedicated blight programs to move these cases. Cleveland's equivalent leverage point is Cleveland Housing Court.

Systematic Sweeps
Commercial corridor inspection program

A five-year program to systematically sweep commercial thoroughfares, rather than responding complaint-by-complaint. Cleveland's corridor analogue would be the Kinsman, Union, Woodland, and St. Clair commercial edges.

Fiscal Tool
Blight tax on designated properties

DeKalb's Board of Commissioners approved blight-tax authority in November 2025, and the county moved to implementation in 2026 — taxing designated blighted properties at up to ten times the standard rate, with revenue restricted to community redevelopment. Rollout targeted an initial tranche of roughly 60 commercial properties.

What Cleveland should not copy

Weak data discipline undermines the record. Investigative reporting by the Atlanta Journal-Constitution found DeKalb's code enforcement database difficult to obtain and described by the county itself as inaccurate and unverified, with a published blight fact sheet that painted a misleading picture of progress. A task force that cannot produce a defensible parcel record cannot demonstrate CDBG national-objective compliance either.

Design implication for the CBTF: the parcel record is a compliance artifact, not an internal convenience. Section 07 specifies an auditable scoring instrument with dated field evidence precisely so that Cleveland's throughput claims survive third-party review.

What does not transfer legally

The blight tax is a Georgia instrument. DeKalb's punitive millage rests on Georgia's constitutional community-redevelopment authority and its implementing statute; Atlanta, Savannah, Clarkston, and Macon-Bibb operate variants of the same mechanism. Ohio has no direct equivalent, and Cleveland should not represent a blight tax as an available tool without an opinion from the Director of Law.

Ohio's functional substitutes — nuisance abatement with cost recovery, building nuisance receivership, land reutilization, and Housing Court enforcement — are set out in Section 13.

Two other structural differences

Governance form. DeKalb operates under a CEO-and-Commission structure in which the CEO can stand up a task force by executive action across county departments. Cleveland's strong-mayor form supports an equivalent move by Executive Order, but Council ratification is recommended to survive administrations.

Jurisdictional scope. DeKalb's program targets unincorporated county territory, a mix weighted toward multifamily complexes and commercial corridors. Cleveland's Ward 8 profile is the inverse — predominantly small residential vacant parcels — which shifts the work from demolition toward stabilization and maintenance.

Proposed Charter — Cleveland Blight Task Force

Charter statement

The Cleveland Blight Task Force is established as a standing multi-agency body responsible for identifying, scoring, sequencing, and resolving blight conditions on every parcel within the City of Cleveland, and for maintaining the authoritative parcel condition record on which all city blight action is based.

Authority and form

AttributeProposed provision
Establishing instrumentMayoral Executive Order, ratified by Council ordinance within 120 days to make the body durable across administrations.
Reporting lineChair reports to the Mayor's Office; quarterly written report to Council's Development, Planning & Sustainability Committee.
FormStanding task force with a permanent operations cell. Not a new department — no charter amendment required.
Directive authoritySets citywide parcel priority and sequencing. Member departments retain their own statutory authority and execute within it; the CBTF cannot override a department's legal determination.
Exclusive authoritySole custodian of the Blight Severity Index and the parcel condition record. No city blight expenditure proceeds on an unscored parcel.
Meeting cadenceOperations cell weekly; full task force monthly; Parcel Review Board biweekly during active CDBG phases.
Sunset / reviewThree-year performance review against Section 12 metrics, with reauthorization by Council.

Scope of jurisdiction

  • Vacant residential parcels — city-owned, Land Bank inventory, and privately held.
  • Post-demolition sites — including demolition scars, unrestored aprons, and orphaned utility cuts.
  • Adjacent public infrastructure — sidewalks, curbs, treelawns, and drainage serving a scored parcel.
  • Illegal dumping sites — on any parcel class, coordinated with county enforcement.
  • Commercial corridor frontages — phased in beginning Year 2, following the DeKalb corridor-sweep model.
  • Excluded: occupied residential structures under active Housing Court jurisdiction, and industrial sites under Ohio EPA remediation orders.

Design note — why a task force and not a department

A new department would require charter change, a lengthy funding fight, and the transfer of statutory authority away from bodies that hold it lawfully today. Every function the CBTF needs already exists somewhere in the city; what is missing is sequencing and a shared record. DeKalb produced substantial blight-removal throughput early on using coordination plus a dedicated appropriation, before it built its property survey. The task force form buys speed now and preserves the option to formalize later.

06

Core Functions

Function 01
Maintain the parcel condition record

Own the authoritative, GIS-linked record of every scored parcel: condition, score, tier, assigned work, responsible department, and closure date. Published to a public dashboard, exported for HUD reporting.

Function 02
Score and tier every parcel

Apply the Blight Severity Index (Section 07) through trained field inspectors with dated photographic evidence. Rescore on a fixed cycle and after any intervention.

Function 03
Sequence and bundle work

Convert scored conditions into single-mobilization work packages that combine debris removal, forestry, grading, turf, and infrastructure repair — eliminating the four-trips-to-one-parcel pattern.

Function 04
Coordinate enforcement

Route dumping evidence to the county Environmental Crimes Task Force, track case status, and hold remediation until evidence collection is complete (Section 08).

Function 05
Drive the judicial pathway

Package private-property cases for the Director of Law and Cleveland Housing Court, following the DeKalb finding that court order precedes private-property action.

Function 06
Set and enforce technical standards

Publish the stabilization specification — grading, soil remediation, turf establishment, erosion control, drainage correction, perimeter safety — and write it into every contractor bid package.

Function 07
Prevent recurrence

Track re-blight at treated parcels, deploy physical deterrents at repeat dumping sites, and route chronic parcels to a reuse pathway rather than a maintenance cycle.

Function 08
Report and comply

Produce quarterly Council reporting, CDBG national-objective documentation, and the audit trail that DeKalb's experience shows is the program's most fragile asset.

Audit discipline

Every score carries the inspector ID, date, GPS fix, and a minimum of four photographs (each elevation plus any scored hazard). Scores older than twelve months expire and the parcel reverts to unscored. This requirement exists because the DeKalb record shows that a task force's throughput claims are only as defensible as the data behind them.

08

Environmental Crimes Coordination Protocol

The Cuyahoga County Environmental Crimes Task Force is the only multi-agency body in the region that directly addresses illegal dumping — the single largest driver of recurring blight on Ward 8 vacant lots. It brings together the City of Cleveland, Cleveland Division of Police, Cuyahoga County Sheriff, County Prosecutor, Board of Health, and the Solid Waste District. It investigates and prosecutes; it does not remediate. The CBTF supplies the missing half.

Source correction — statutory citation

The Packet 2 draft cites ORC 2927.22 as the felony illegal-dumping statute. That citation is incorrect and should be replaced before submission. The controlling provisions are:

ORC 3734.03 — prohibits disposal of solid waste by open burning or open dumping.
ORC 3734.99 — a reckless violation is an unclassified felony, punishable by a fine of $10,000–$25,000, imprisonment of two to four years, or both.
ORC 3767.32 — littering and unauthorized deposit of litter on public or private property; misdemeanor exposure with court-ordered cleanup.

The body is also correctly named the Cuyahoga County Environmental Crimes Task Force, not the Cleveland-Cuyahoga County Environmental Crimes Task Force. Both corrections are carried into this document.

Ward 8 dumping profile

  • Construction debris deposited on cleared residential parcels
  • Household waste accumulation at unmonitored interior-block lots
  • Scrap tire piles — a distinct Ohio EPA enforcement category
  • Contractor dumping following demolition activity
  • Hazardous materials abandonment

Why the evidence hold matters

Under the current arrangement, cleanup crews routinely destroy the evidence that would support a felony prosecution — the exact failure described as Gap 5. A 24-hour evidence hold with a 10-day decision window preserves the case without materially delaying remediation, and it is the single cheapest structural fix in this framework.

Ward 8 — The Operational Pilot

The Ward 8 Vacant-Lot Stabilization & Infrastructure Remediation Program is already scoped, budgeted, and eligible. It functions as a proto-task-force today: it performs standardized parcel scoring, coordinated debris removal, sidewalk and curb remediation, forestry hazard mitigation, turf establishment, drainage correction, contractor bid specification, engineering-grade technical standards, GIS-ready mapping, and compliance documentation — across departmental lines, under one program.

Designating Ward 8 as the CBTF pilot converts an isolated grant program into the proving ground for a citywide system, at no additional program cost.

Pilot terms
  • Duration: 18 months, matching the CDBG work plan's four phases.
  • Coverage: approximately 120–150 vacant parcels across the five Hough interior priority zones.
  • Reporting: the CBTF Operations Cell produces the program's HUD documentation, testing the compliance workflow at real scale.
  • Decision gate: at month 12, Council reviews Section 12 metrics and authorizes citywide extension or revision.

Funding note — read before drafting the budget narrative

CDBG program administration and planning costs are subject to the 20% cap at 24 CFR 570.200(g). Only the portion of CBTF cost properly allocable to eligible planning and administrative activity may be charged to CDBG, and it competes with the city's existing administrative draw. The realistic structure is a blend:

  • CDBG administration and planning — parcel survey and condition-record development attributable to eligible planning activity, within the cap
  • CDBG activity delivery — inspection and coordination costs directly delivering eligible clearance and public improvement activities
  • City general fund — the coordinating core that cannot be tied to a specific eligible activity
  • Cuyahoga County Solid Waste District — dumping deterrence, scrap tire, and litter-abatement components
  • Cost recovery — nuisance abatement assessments and Land Bank disposition proceeds, growing from Year 2

Ohio Legal Basis

Cleveland cannot import DeKalb's blight tax. It can assemble equivalent leverage from Ohio instruments already available. Each provision below requires confirmation by the Director of Law before it is relied upon in a submission or an enforcement action.

InstrumentProvisionApplication
Nuisance abatement & cost recoveryORC 715.26, ORC 715.261Municipal authority to regulate and remove insecure, unsafe, or structurally defective buildings, with abatement costs recoverable as an assessment against the property. The closest functional analogue to DeKalb's fiscal pressure.
Building nuisance receivershipORC 3767.41Civil action to declare a building a public nuisance and appoint a receiver to abate it. The strongest tool against absentee owners of structures.
Land reutilizationORC Chapter 5722Acquisition, holding, and disposition of nonproductive land through the land reutilization program — the reuse pathway for chronic parcels.
Illegal dumpingORC 3734.03 / ORC 3734.99Open dumping prohibited; reckless violation is an unclassified felony with a $10,000–$25,000 fine and two to four years imprisonment. Basis of the enforcement referral in Section 08.
LitteringORC 3767.32Unauthorized deposit of litter on public or private property; misdemeanor exposure with court-ordered cleanup. Applies to lower-volume incidents below the felony threshold.
Judicial venueCleveland Housing CourtDedicated venue for code and nuisance enforcement. The functional counterpart to DeKalb's Magistrate Court blight program and the CBTF's escalation route.
Establishing authorityCleveland Charter — executive and legislative powersMayoral Executive Order to establish; Council ordinance to ratify and appropriate. Form and citation to be confirmed by the Director of Law.
Legal review required

This section identifies candidate instruments and is not a legal opinion. The Director of Law should confirm the scope of each provision, the correct establishing instrument under the Charter, and whether any Ohio mechanism supports differential taxation of designated blighted property, before any representation of available authority is made to HUD or to Council.

The argument this framework adds

A grant application says: this ward has blighted lots and we would like to fix them. This framework says: the city has no mechanism to identify, prioritize, or resolve blight anywhere, this project builds that mechanism, and the ward is where it gets proven. The second argument is materially stronger under a slum-and-blight national objective, because it establishes the area-basis condition rather than asserting it.
